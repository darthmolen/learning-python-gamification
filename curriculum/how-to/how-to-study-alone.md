# How to work on your own

Most of this curriculum is built for two people at a table. That is not politeness — somebody
sitting beside you who asks the right question is the single best thing this design has, and no
page can replace it.

But there will be evenings when nobody is free and you want to work anyway. This is how to do
that well, and how to tell the difference between the parts that survive being done alone and
the parts that do not.

## What you can do alone, and what you cannot

**These work perfectly alone.** Reading the lesson. Working an exercise from its brief. Running
your code and reading what it says. Looking a word up. Writing in your journal. Going back over
something from three weeks ago because you have a suspicion about it.

**These do not.** A practice with no files in it. About one in five has none. The work in
those is a conversation: you predict out loud what a program will do
before running it, you explain what broke and why, you argue about which of two ideas is better.
None of that fits in a folder, and reading about it is not doing it.

**Leave those where they are.** Do not skip them and do not try to do them silently. Mark where
you got to and pick them up with somebody. If you skip them you will hit the work that comes
after and find something missing, and you will not know what.

## The order still matters

Work in order. It is the one rule of sequence in the whole curriculum and it is real — number 4
assumes you did number 3, and the person who wrote it knew what you would already have seen.

`practices.yml` in each area folder is that order, written down. It is the list, and it is the
same list whether anybody is watching.

## What to do when you are stuck and there is nobody to ask

The ladder normally comes from the person beside you. Alone, you walk it yourself. It is slower
and it works.

1. **Read what the computer said. All of it.** Not the last line — the whole thing. It is telling
   you where it got to and what it could not do. You will get very good at this, and it will save
   you more time than anything else in here.

2. **Say out loud what you expected, then what happened.** Out loud, actually — not in your head.
   About half the time the answer is somewhere in the middle of that sentence, and you only hear
   it when you say it.

3. **Make it smaller.** Delete everything that is not the broken part. Keep deleting until it
   either works or the smallest possible broken thing is in front of you. Then you are looking at
   the actual problem instead of at fifty lines around it.

4. **Print something.** You are guessing what a value is. Stop guessing. Put a `print` on the
   line above and find out.

5. **Look it up.** Every idea has a page in the field manual, from the very start, whether you
   have met it yet or not. Nothing is hidden until you have earned it.

6. **Stop, and write down where you got to.** This is a real step and not a failure. Ninety
   minutes of grinding teaches less than twenty minutes and a good night's sleep, and a note
   saying *"stuck here, I think it is the loop"* is worth more tomorrow than whatever you would
   have typed at minute ninety.

**Notice what is not on that list: someone telling you the answer.** It was not on the list when
there was somebody in the room either. What they would have given you is a better question, and
one through five are those questions, written down.

## Keep a list of what you could not work out

Not a list of failures — a list for the next time somebody *is* free. Two lines each:

> I could not get the countdown to stop at 1 instead of 0. I tried changing the range twice and
> the second one made it worse.

That is a good half-hour of somebody's time aimed exactly where it helps, instead of them asking
"how is it going?" and you saying "fine". You are not reporting a problem. You are handing over a
question you have already done the work on, which is a skill in its own right and one you will
use for the rest of your life.

## Do not read ahead into the answers

Some of what is in this curriculum is deliberately not on your machine, and one of those things
is the worked answers.

There is a legal way to see one — two real attempts and a written sentence about what you tried
first — and it exists because looking is not the problem. **Hiding that you looked is the
problem**, because it destroys the only signal anybody has about what you actually know, and then
the next thing you get handed is the wrong difficulty.

Alone, nobody is checking. That is exactly why it is worth doing properly. The rule is not there
to catch you.

## Write it down at the end

Whatever kind of evening it was, finish the same way: what you made, what broke, what you would
do differently, and what you think will break next time.

That last one matters more than it sounds. You are practicing noticing trouble before it
arrives — and when you work alone, your own written record is the only thing standing in for the
person who would otherwise have asked.

Your journal is the part of all this that is most durably yours. Long after the rest of it is
gone, it will still be a file in your own repository, in your own words.

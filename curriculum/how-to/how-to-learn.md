# How to learn

This page is about the curriculum — the Python, and the order it comes in. It is not about
the game. Nothing here stops being true if the game is switched off tomorrow.

## What a practice is

A **practice** is one unit of the work. It has a number and a name, like *Practice 3 — The
Broken Sigil*, and the practices in an area run in order from 1.

A practice is not a length of time. It is an amount of work. Some you will finish in one
sitting and some will take you two or three, and neither of those means anything is wrong.
The number tells you where you are in the order, and that is all it tells you.

You work practices in order. That is the one rule of sequence in the whole curriculum, and it
is real — Practice 4 assumes you did Practice 3, and the person who wrote it knew what you
would already have seen.

## What an exercise is

Some practices come with **exercises**. An exercise is a piece of work with its own folder,
holding three things.

- A **brief**, which is the instructions. It is written to you, and it tells you what the
  finished thing has to do.
- A **starter**, which is a file to begin from. Sometimes it is nearly empty. Sometimes it is
  deliberately broken, and fixing it is the point.
- A **hidden test**, which checks the finished thing. You do not read it before you write your
  answer, for the same reason you do not read the back of the book first.

An exercise belongs to the practice that teaches it. One exercise can belong to two practices
when it is built out of both — you will meet that, and the brief will say so.

## Why some practices have no exercises

Plenty of practices have no exercise folder at all, and those are not lesser practices.

The work in them happens at the table, out loud, with somebody else. You predict what a
program will do before you run it. You explain what went wrong and why. You draw something and
argue about how to make it better. None of that fits in a folder, and none of it can be
checked by a machine, which is exactly why a person is sitting next to you.

**A practice with no files is still a practice, and doing it is not optional.** If you skip
them you will hit the ones that come after and find that something you were supposed to have
is missing.

## What to do when you are stuck

Being stuck is not a failure state. It is most of programming.

1. **Read what the computer said.** All of it, not just the last line. It is telling you where
   it got to and what it could not do. You will get very good at this, and it will save you
   more time than anything else in here.
2. **Say out loud what you expected to happen.** Then say what happened instead. About half
   the time you will find the answer somewhere in the middle of that sentence.
3. **Look it up in the field manual.** Every idea has a page, from the very start, whether you
   have met it yet or not. Nothing is hidden until you have earned it.
4. **Ask.** There is somebody sitting there for this.

## Writing things down

At the end of a practice you write in your journal. It is one file, it is yours, and you keep
adding to the bottom of it.

Write what you made, what you would do next, and what you think might break. That last one
matters more than it sounds — you are practicing the thing where you notice trouble before it
arrives, and it is a habit that follows you out of here into every project you ever work on.

The journal is the part of all this that is most durably yours. Long after the rest of this
is gone, it will still be a file in your own repository, in your own words.
